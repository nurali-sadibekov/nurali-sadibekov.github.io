In my thermodynamics course, I studied entropy and its role in the second law of thermodynamics, which states that the total entropy of an isolated system never decreases. A local increase in order is therefore possible only if it is compensated by a larger increase in disorder elsewhere.

Consider tidying a room. Once the books are shelved and the clothes put away, the room is clearly more ordered. Thermodynamically, however, the process is not free: the body metabolizes food and releases heat into the surroundings, increasing the entropy of the environment by more than the room’s entropy decreased. The total entropy of the universe still rises.

A similar logic can be applied, as an analogy, to living organisms. A developing child is a highly organized structure, built and sustained by the mother’s energy and resources, which are drawn from food and ultimately dissipated as heat and waste. Throughout life, every organism maintains its own order only by continuously consuming energy and exporting entropy to its surroundings.

Entropy is therefore not only a concept of physics but a useful lens for understanding why order, in any form, always has a cost.
